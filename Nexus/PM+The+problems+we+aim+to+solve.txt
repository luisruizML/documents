PM The problems we aim to solve
1. Citizens lose time they can’t afford to lose.
Technology should make life simpler, yet many government services are still designed around institutional constraints rather than citizens’ lives. The result is familiar: repeated information requests, long queues, fragmented services, and complex forms for even the simplest tasks, from applying for benefits to paying taxes or requesting permits. Time is wasted, confidence drops, and essential interactions with the state feel harder than they should, discouraging participation and trust.
2. Institutions operate with inefficiencies that drain capacity and morale.
Public servants work with outdated systems, manual tasks, low visibility into their workload, and large backlogs that make it hard to deliver timely services. This not only reduces their impact; it also redirects institutional energy toward administrative effort instead of public value. Compliance suffers, satisfaction drops, and resources are locked into activities that add little value.
3. Data is an untapped asset and a barrier instead of an enabler.
Governments increasingly recognize data as a strategic asset, yet most institutions are unable to use it effectively. Siloed systems, inconsistent standards, and limited data literacy prevent information from flowing across agencies. When data cannot move securely and reliably, services cannot be seamless, decisions remain reactive, and policies fail to adapt to real needs.
4. Trust between citizens and institutions is fragile.
Trust is one of the most valuable assets a public institution can have, yet it is often undermined by opaque processes, limited transparency, and poor traceability of decisions. When citizens do not understand how obligations are calculated, how data is used, or where public resources go, perceptions of unfairness grow. This loss of trust directly reduces compliance, participation, and the effectiveness of public services.
5. Internally, we face scaling and quality constraints rooted in our legacy ways of working.
Being historically project-oriented has led to architectural fragmentation, duplicated efforts, and teams aligned to clients rather than outcomes. This creates scalability limitations, inconsistent quality, and a heavy dependency on custom development, preventing us from dedicating our talent to building high-impact capabilities that benefit multiple countries.
